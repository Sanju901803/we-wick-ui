Canada's justice system ensures that every person is protected by the law and entitled to fair treatment. At its core is the presumption of innocence — in any criminal matter, a person is considered innocent unless and until proven guilty.

The foundation of Canadian law rests on four key principles: the rule of law, freedom under the law, democratic principles, and due process. Due process means the government is bound to uphold all legal rights that individuals are entitled to.

Canadian society is shaped by a comprehensive framework of written laws created by elected representatives. Courts resolve disputes, while the police are responsible for upholding those laws. Importantly, the law applies equally to all — politicians, judges, and police officers included. These laws exist to maintain social order, offer peaceful means of resolving conflict, and reflect the shared values of Canadians.

Courts

At the top of Canada's court hierarchy sits the Supreme Court of Canada, the final authority on all legal matters. The Federal Court of Canada handles cases involving the federal government. Most provinces have both an appeal court and a trial court — sometimes known as the Court of Queen's Bench or Superior Court. Below these are provincial courts that handle less serious offences, along with specialized courts for family matters, traffic violations, and small civil claims.

Police

Police in Canada are dedicated to public safety and law enforcement. They are available to assist in a wide range of situations — from accidents and theft to assaults, crimes in progress, or missing persons.

Canada has multiple layers of policing. Ontario and Quebec have their own provincial police forces, and most municipalities maintain local police departments. The Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP) enforces federal legislation across the country and also acts as the provincial police service in all provinces and territories outside Ontario and Quebec, including some municipalities. The police are always available to assist you.

If you have concerns about how you were treated by police, you have the right to raise them. Nearly every Canadian police force has a formal complaint process that allows members of the public to file concerns and seek a response.

Getting Legal Help

A lawyer can advise you on legal issues and represent you in court. If the cost of legal representation is a barrier, legal aid services are available in most communities at little or no cost.
